An AI Invented a Co-Founder for My Company. So I Built a Detector.

Lindsay Hiebert · August 2026 · 5-minute read

Someone asked an AI assistant who runs AEO Analyzers. It answered confidently — and gave me a co-founder. I read it twice. I don't have one. The person it named is real and has no affiliation with my company, my product, or me. The engine hadn't set out to deceive anyone; it had assembled an identity out of fragments and filled a gap with a plausible name.

That is a stranger thing to see than a wrong number. A number is a measurement error. A person is a fabricated relationship — a fact about my company that never happened, delivered to a buyer in the same calm voice as everything true.

Why an engine invents a person

Answer engines build a picture of "who" from scattered third-party sources. When the first-party record is thin or ambiguous, the model interpolates — and interpolation applied to people invents relationships. It's the same mechanism that fuses my product with a stock ticker (AEO is a listed apparel company) and with browser extensions that share its letters: entities that look alike get merged, and the seams get filled with confident guesses.

Being crawled doesn't protect you from this. As I wrote in Part 2, the engines had already visited my site hundreds of times. Reading isn't understanding. A page can be fully indexed and still leave "who made this, and with whom" open enough for a model to answer it wrong — and then repeat the wrong answer, because it's now the most fluent version it has.

The embarrassment became a measurement

I could have written to the engine and asked for a correction. Instead I built the check into the product. Every branded answer AEO Analyzers collects is now compared against the site's own record and classified: accurate, drifted, or confused with a named colliding entity — and when it's confused, the report names the entity the model has fused onto you, so you can see exactly which stranger you've been merged with. The invented co-founder is what taught me that fidelity is a separate measurement from visibility. You can be found perfectly and described wrongly, and a tool that only reports a visibility score will tell you everything is fine while a fabricated fact circulates.

The fix is a first-party record, stated plainly

Models resolve ambiguity toward the most consistent record they can find. So I gave them one. Structured data that names the sole creator and states, in plain language, that there are no co-founders. A one-sentence disambiguation repeated in both the schema and the visible page. An entity graph that separates the product from the tickers and tools that share its letters. And a neutral public anchor in a knowledge base the engines already trust. None of this forces a correction — nobody honest can promise that. What it does is starve the fabrication of the ambiguity it fed on.

What mine says now

Is it fixed? Branded fidelity is better, and I still catch the occasional collision — and when I do, it's in the report: named, dated, with the transcript attached. I can't make an engine stop. I can measure what it says, correct the record it reads, and re-check every month, in public, whatever the result. That's the whole method — not a promise the machine is right about you, but an honest, reproducible read of when it isn't, and a fix path that's actually yours to control.
